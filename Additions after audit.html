01 / The opportunity

The premise already has an advantage.
The decisions need to catch up.

Creating something, watching time change it, then investigating what remains is the game’s strongest teaching idea. Extend that relationship until the player must predict, test, interpret and explain.

6 clipsNarration files in the manifest
0 tracksMusic files in the manifest

Preserve what is distinctive

  • The player’s actual painting and pot mark survive into the excavation.
  • The missing basket makes preservation bias personal.
  • The timeline is reversible, has no year zero and already includes animated, user-paced teaching.
  • The evidence board requires sources and supports “cannot tell”.
  • Offline play, durable artwork saves, a notebook and an exportable field journal support classroom use.

The design test

For every addition, finish this sentence:

“The learner now has to use this historical idea to make this decision, and the world shows why it matters.”

If the answer is only “it looks more exciting”, treat it as atmosphere. Atmosphere can support attention and attachment, but it does not establish understanding.

ACT 1 / LIVE

From errands to causes

Keep the tactile world and shared tools. Add small choices about where to plant, what materials do, why people exchange goods and how a community responds to scarcity.

ACT 2 / PASS

From a dial to experiments

Keep the strong time controls and bridge. Make change observable, compare conditions and turn calendars and deep time into manipulable models.

ACT 3 / DIG

From reveals to reasoning

Keep the emotional recovery. Make specialists perform distinct investigations, give science a question and end with evidence from a past the player does not already know.

02 / What the book asks

The benchmark includes inquiry,
not just remembering facts.

The supplied chapter asks three big questions: how historical time is measured, how sources reveal the past, and how early humans lived. The book also asks students to interpret unfamiliar objects, construct timelines, trace family information to sources, question reconstructions and investigate beyond the classroom. A fair “better than the book” claim must include these activities, not compare the game only against passive reading.

03 / Code & learning findings

Fix the weak foundations
before increasing the spectacle.

Severity reflects impact on access, teaching validity and the proposed expansion. These are a mixture of source-confirmed issues, observed behavior and design limitations; the text distinguishes them. Automated green checks do not validate pedagogy or complete accessibility.

F01HighLearning validity

One correct answer becomes mastery

A correct choice calls master(). Results count a concept as retrieved whenever correct is greater than zero. Immediate retries and answers after explanations are not distinguished from independent, delayed retrieval. The report already separates taught, asked and retrieved, which is valuable; the remaining problem is what those measures can establish.

Recommended change. Keep successful practice encouraging. Separately record first attempt, hints, feedback exposure, question variant, delay and transfer. Reserve durable mastery for repeated independent success across occasions. Never infer a learning advantage from the existing counter alone.

Acceptance check. Give one wrong answer, read its explanation and answer correctly. The learning record must retain that assistance, even if the story progresses.

recall.js:51–74; codex.js:124–130; save.js:242–253; results.js:61–95

F02HighAccessibility

Required creative tasks have no keyboard completion path

Cave painting and the pot mark require at least one canvas stroke. Strokes increment through pointer events. The panels do not use the shared activity() focus/input owner. Pot presets help shape selection, but the following mark step still requires a pointer. This is a source-confirmed barrier, not a completed screen-reader audit.

Recommended change. Use activity() for every craft panel. Add named colour controls, keyboard stamp placement, a small selectable mark grid and accessible preset alternatives. Preserve original-player artwork and creative choice.

Acceptance check. Complete painting, shaping and marking using only keyboard, then recover the same artwork in Act 3. Verify focus and world-input restoration.

ui/paint.js:60–163 and 167 onward; ui/pot.js:18 onward; acts/act1.js:1013 and 1550

F03HighAccessibility

The focus trap skips evidence disclosures

activity() overrides Tab navigation but its control selector excludes summary. The evidence board and chapter notebook use native details/summary controls for inspection and explanation. Keyboard Tab therefore skips precisely the evidence that students are supposed to inspect.

Recommended change. Use a complete, visibility-aware focusable-element query, including summary, links and appropriate tabindex values. Cover nested dialogs and disabled/hidden controls. This is a code finding; add a targeted keyboard reproduction before merging a fix.

Acceptance check. Tab to an evidence summary, open it with Enter or Space, read the evidence, choose a verdict, close the dialog and return to the original focus.

ui/activity.js:21–29; ui/report.js:231–238; ui/chapterNotebook.js:55–56

F04HighLearning design

Watching the decisive change is not required

The preservation phase completes after the timeline is more than 5,200 years beyond the start. In the live check, entering 100 BCE from 5400 BCE immediately triggered the basket-gone narration. The free-scrub phase similarly tests an endpoint; its wrapper marks era and Ice Age concepts taught without requiring their individual landmark explanations.

Recommended change. Retain exact-date freedom, but replay a compact before/change/after comparison when a jump skips the key transition. Record a concept exposure at its actual teaching event. Ask the player to identify the changed object or explain the missing evidence.

Acceptance check. A large jump, reverse scrub and resumed session must all offer the essential observation; optional discoveries should remain optional and separate from mastery.

acts/act2.js:377–396, 467–475 and 205–214; sky/erosion.js:35 onward

F05MediumLearning design

Scientific analysis is three reveal buttons

The lab offers grain, charcoal and bone. Each button waits 900 ms and reveals a fixed text result. The player does not choose a question, analytical method or interpretation. Ancient climate is mentioned in the lab note but has no corresponding interactive sample. Coverage exists as text; the scientific reasoning is underdeveloped.

Recommended change. Start with a historical question. Choose a suitable sample and specialist analysis, inspect a simplified result, then update a claim. Add a climate evidence example. Show date ranges and limits, not an instrument that magically supplies a whole story.

Acceptance check. Given an unfamiliar sample, identify a question the analysis can answer and one it cannot. A genetics result must never be treated as proof of language, religion or exact beliefs.

ui/report.js:269–317; acts/act3.js:764–770; strings.js:696–708

F06MediumVisual explanation

Deep time is described as tiny but drawn large

The prose explicitly labels the display logarithmic, so it is not an unlabelled-axis defect. However, the highlighted human segment takes about 37.2% of the logarithmic bar. Using the game’s dates, 300,000 years is about 0.0066% of Earth’s 4.54-billion-year history. The picture can dominate the intended “tiny fraction” message. Live inspection also found dense explanatory text over the existing world and panels.

Recommended change. Begin with a true-scale whole-Earth bar, then visibly magnify the recent portion. Label each zoom and preserve a locator on the full bar. Let the student reverse the zoom. Integrate the panel with activity() so initial focus moves to the lesson.

Acceptance check. Ask which is longer, all human history or an earlier geological span, using a new scale. The answer must not depend on recalling the current bar’s proportions.

acts/act2.js:405–464; strings.js:583–584 and 1018

F07MediumGameplay

Several actions train compliance more than reasoning

Planting uses four fixed points; the dispute and lean week are observed; refusing the barter loops until acceptance; knapping and drilling share a timing ring; digs count fixed layers; basket creation is one interaction. These are functioning story beats, but their completion does not require most of the historical reasoning named afterward.

Recommended change. Replace selected repetitions with small, bounded decisions about materials, locations, community needs and evidence. Keep motor assistance and safe retries. A useful failure should expose a wrong model of the subject, not slow reflexes.

Acceptance check. For each new mechanic, state the historical idea the learner must use to succeed. If pressing the highlighted action is enough, strengthen the decision or shorten the beat.

acts/act1.js:989–1001, 1066–1078, 1098–1108, 1342–1375, 1438–1458, 1573–1580; acts/act3.js:319–381

F08MediumRetention

Review is not a persistent spaced-practice schedule

Misses are queued only in memory, retried at a later slot and flushed at act end. There is no due date or minimum spacing interval. practiceRecall() takes the first eligible entries with slice(0, count). After all are mastered, the same early entries can dominate repeated practice. The comments describe stronger spacing than the implementation guarantees.

Recommended change. Persist due dates, last attempt, variant and support level. Interleave due concepts across acts. Prioritize weak and overdue concepts, then rotate established ones. Offer a short return expedition after a delay, without requiring notifications or a server.

Acceptance check. Reload after a miss; the concept remains due. Run several review sessions; later concepts receive practice. A just-explained answer is not labelled delayed retrieval.

recall.js:35, 85–112

F09MediumAudio readiness

The audio engine is ahead of its content and lesson integration

The manifest lists six narration clips, eight NPC clips and no music. The scanner reports 108 detected text lines, with three recorded and 105 missing; this is not a complete census because it scans selected call patterns. Time lessons, discoveries and interview talkLine() use custom UI without a narration playback path. Ambient wind and bird synthesis already exist. Music tracks alone will not create an adaptive score.

Recommended change. Add a consistent caption/voice interface to all teaching surfaces and explicit voice IDs for generated text. Prioritize consequential explanations. Add contextual sound zones and automatic music/ambience reduction under speech. Use per-track gains before introducing music crossfades; current stopMusic() and playMusic() share one gain bus and a delayed reset.

Acceptance check. Check every teaching panel with voice on, sound off, replay, background/return and unavailable files. Audit offline first-use behavior; cached code does not imply all audio is cached.

audio/manifest.json; audio.js:31–83; sound.js:72–85, 296–382; sky/timeLessons.js; sky/discoveries.js; acts/act3.js:732–749

F10MediumMotion and access

Reduced motion is partial, not a global presentation policy

The newer lessons respect reduced motion, and gentleCamera removes some player-camera effects. Scripted ascent/descent tweens and world particle effects do not consult the same setting. Adding more cinematic motion would widen this inconsistency.

Recommended change. Create one motion policy for camera travel, shakes, flashes, particles and UI animation. Provide a static end-state plus explanatory cue when motion is reduced. Essential evidence must remain available without animation or sound.

Acceptance check. Complete all acts with reduced motion, larger text and muted audio. Specifically inspect time jumps, grave discovery, era waves and camera descent, not only CSS transitions.

settings.js:14–17; player/player.js:391 and 426; sky/timeLessons.js; sky/interstitial.js:55–90; acts/act2.js:594–608; fx/fx.js

F11MediumAssessment

Knowing your own past can bypass historical inference

Recognising the player’s own objects is the game’s emotional advantage. It is also an assessment confound: the player already knows what happened. The four fixed claims require selected evidence, which is a real improvement, but cannot by themselves show transfer to an unknown past.

Recommended change. Keep the autobiographical excavation. Add one small, unfamiliar site with a different evidence pattern and multiple plausible reconstructions. Separate “I remember doing this” from “these traces support this conclusion.”

Acceptance check. With no Act 1 memory to rely on, the player chooses evidence, rejects an overconfident explanation and names what remains unknown.

acts/act3.js:383–595; ui/report.js:208–264

F12LowMaintenance

Documentation and checks do not describe the same generation of game

HANDOFF still describes the former timed no-zero task and old controls; parts of COVERAGE retain “TRICK challenge” labels even though newer lessons are user paced. Pot shaping contains an English instruction outside strings.js despite the documented centralization rule. The current lint passes, so that invariant is not fully enforced.

Recommended change. Update active handoff instructions and coverage names. Expand the text audit to catch inline teaching copy. Keep historical changelog entries clearly historical.

Acceptance check. A new contributor following only the current handoff should reach the current controls and lessons without undoing completed improvements.

HANDOFF.md; game/COVERAGE.md entries 4.13–4.15; ui/pot.js:31; tools/lint-strings.mjs

Where 3D video helps

Use short, chaptered clips for a real excavation workflow, close-up experimental craft, or a museum object/process the current world cannot show clearly. Place a prediction before the clip and a decision after it. Give playback controls, captions, a transcript, a poster and still-frame fallback.

A 360° museum visit is an optional extension when spatial context matters. It is not the first investment. For the pot, layers, calendar and preservation tray, the existing Three.js scene can usually provide a more useful interactive model than a fixed video.

Where more media will not help

Do not add an uninterrupted documentary between acts, simultaneous voice/music/text/effects competing for attention, generic confetti for every find, or photorealistic invented reconstructions presented as evidence.

Review the grave discovery carefully: the shared dig completion currently emits confetti even after the solemn burial reveal. An object-specific response would keep emotional tone coherent.

Audio production order

  1. Comprehension: narration for core relationships, with replay and captions on every relevant panel. Add explicit playback support to lessons, discoveries and interviews.
  2. Action feedback: distinct clay, reed, shell, stone, water and soil sounds tied to events the player can see.
  3. Place: a small number of spatial/zone-based sound sources, cave filtering and active camp sounds. Preserve intelligibility and a complete muted route.
  4. Emotion: sparse object motifs and music states once independent gains, ducking and pause/resume are reliable.

The names of the existing NPC audio assets suggest a caricature risk, but this audit did not perform a listening review of every recording. Audition them with the intended portrayal in mind. The chapter describes early humans as capable social people with rich languages now lost; no reconstruction should imply that their speech was known or inherently simplistic.

A practical media contract

Each asset should name the concept it supports, trigger, duration or control, caption/transcript, reduced-motion alternative, offline behavior, quality tier, source/illustrative status and cleanup owner. Use original or appropriately licensed media. Do not bundle scanned textbook pages as game art; source figures were inspected locally for this audit.

06 / First playable upgrade

Build the pot-and-basket experiment first.

It improves all three acts, uses the game’s own objects, and gives animation, audio and assessment one shared purpose.

Make and use

Keep the custom pot and mark. Add a brief basket weave and one useful action with it. The player places both objects together; save their exact visual identity and use.

Predict

Ask what a future excavator will find and why. Save the first prediction without treating it as a graded failure. Show the same shelf, not a generic diagram disconnected from the player’s objects.

See the change

Let the player advance, stop and reverse. Emphasise fraying, collapse, burial and chipping. When a large jump skips the sequence, offer a short replay of the important difference.

Compare one condition

Open a labelled side experiment with a contrasting preservation condition. Change only that condition and compare outcomes. Keep the canonical story site stable. Avoid presenting illustrative years as measured decay constants.

Recover and reason

Excavate with layer/context recording. Inspect the marked pot and the absence of a basket. Reject “they made pottery but never wove baskets” using the observed limits of preservation.

Transfer

Present a new site with an unfamiliar missing organic object. Ask for a defensible claim, supporting evidence and what cannot be known. Repeat an equivalent case after a delay.

Definition of done

Original artefacts remain recognisable across save/reload and act changes. The decisive change is understandable with no sound or animation. The player can complete it by keyboard or touch. Wrong predictions receive a causal explanation. A new-case answer is recorded separately from supported story practice. No main-world terrain-height changes are required.

Performance and regression gates

  • Use the existing 30 fps target as the baseline. Measure frame-time percentiles, draw calls, memory and loading on an actual low-end phone before setting additional media budgets. The enlarged world makes old performance numbers insufficient.
  • Prefer procedural transforms, reused geometry, instancing and pooled particles. Avoid a new renderer or a second full world for each cutaway.
  • Load a detailed model only when inspected; dispose its resources on exit. Pause background simulation or rendering during full-screen video when safe.
  • Test the original art round-trip, activity interruption, save quota failure, offline first visit to a lesson, hidden-tab return and wrong-answer recovery.
  • Check desktop, small phone portrait, short landscape, larger text, keyboard, reduced motion and mute. Browser viewport checks do not replace physical touch/device testing.

08 / Prove the advantage

“Better than reading”
needs a defined comparison.

The current build has a strong hypothesis, not evidence of superiority. Make the claim about a named chapter, learner group, learning outcome and amount of time.

A claim to aim for

“For Class 6 learners studying Timeline and Sources of History, this version of the game improved delayed retention and application to unfamiliar evidence compared with a time-matched textbook-and-activities condition.”

This is a proposed future claim. It is not a finding of this audit. Any published claim must report the actual study, comparison, uncertainty and limits.

OutcomeA useful taskWhat the current counters miss
Conceptual understandingExplain why BCE-to-CE gaps subtract one; why missing wood does not prove non-useCorrect button selection need not reveal the explanation
RetentionEquivalent fresh questions immediately and after 7–14 daysNo durable due schedule; repeated known options

Efficiency and accessCompare gains per total session time; track onboarding, help and completionActive foreground time includes more than concept learning

A credible classroom evaluation

  1. Usability first: observe a small, varied group of Class 6 learners. Watch where they misunderstand, miss an animation, cannot read a panel or need control help. This can diagnose problems but cannot establish superiority.
  2. Define outcomes before collecting the comparison: choose delayed retention and unfamiliar-evidence transfer as primary measures; use a common rubric and equivalent test forms. Set sample size from the effect worth detecting, variation and classroom clustering, rather than claiming a small pilot is conclusive.
  3. Compare fairly: assign learners or classes to game versus the supplied chapter with its activities, with matched time and support. Include onboarding in the game’s practical time cost. Balance prior knowledge, reading level and gaming familiarity where possible.
  4. Assess independently: use a pretest, immediate test and delayed test without the game or book open. Include short explanations and unseen cases. Have scorers use the same rubric without knowing the study condition where feasible.
  5. Report the whole result: group sizes, assignment, completion, missing follow-ups, uncertainty, learning gains, time and usability differences. Do not drop struggling non-completers and then claim broad superiority.
  6. Learn what helped: after the main comparison, test selected features with and without the addition. This separates the value of the preservation interaction, narration or explanation prompts from the novelty of playing a game.

Three transfer tasks ready to author

UNKNOWN SITE

No basket found

Two sites have pottery. One also has preserved woven material. Ask which claims about weaving are supported and what preservation information is missing.

UNKNOWN ACCOUNT

Three voices, one source

Three people repeat a story; two cite the same storyteller and one cites a different document. Ask which evidence is independent and what still needs checking.

UNKNOWN OBJECT

A marked fragment

A sherd has a repeated symbol and no translation. Ask what is observed, what can be inferred and what would be needed to interpret its meaning.

Reward careful explanations and productive revision inside the game. Keep the external assessment separate so learning support does not contaminate the evidence used for the comparison. Optional short written or teacher-recorded spoken explanations can capture reasoning; there is no need to automatically judge free-form answers as correct.

Research informing the recommendations

The feature proposals above are design inferences applied to this codebase. The sources below support the learning principles, not a claim that these specific additions have already improved this game.

  1. Institute of Education Sciences: Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student LearningSupports spaced learning, retrieval, worked-example/problem alternation, graphics with verbal explanation, concrete/abstract links and explanatory questions. Applied here to return expeditions, visible models and reasoning checks.
  2. Wouters & van Oostendorp (2013): Instructional support in game-based learningThe meta-analysis reports benefits of instructional support in games, with limitations including publication-bias evidence.   3. Mayer: Segmenting, pretraining and modality principlesDescribes learner-paced segments and managing the demands of complex narrated animation. Applied here to pause/scrub/replay, concise speech and visuals focused on a relationship.

The supplied textbook is the curriculum authority for this audit. Research supports the instructional direction, while whether this game outperforms the book remains an empirical question. Production details and historical reconstructions still need content review.
